Religion — Learn the Bibles, and All Religions, Cross-Referenced

For people who want to learn more about the Bible — and about other faiths, in their own terms. Start simple, then go deeper. This page does not merge traditions into one teaching, and it does not present comic fiction as scripture. Real-world learning here; Captain Infinity fiction stays in its own lane, as in About.

We turn complexity into elementary: a canon is the list of books a community accepts as Scripture. The difference between Bibles is almost entirely in the Old Testament. Most Christian traditions share the same 27-book New Testament. Counting also changes totals — the same writings can be counted as one book or split into two.

1. Pick a Bible canon

* Ethiopian total is the commonly cited 81. Grouping varies: narrower 54 + 27, broader 46 + 35 with church-order texts. Eastern Orthodox totals vary by church and counting. The Book of Mormon is separate — Latter-day Saint scripture used alongside the Bible, not a Bible canon.

Tap to open each canon, in plain terms

Tanakh (24): Torah 5, Nevi'im 8 counted, Ketuvim 11. Judaism's scripture; Christians call much of it the Old Testament.

Protestant (66): 39 Old Testament + 27 New Testament. Baptist, Methodist, Lutheran, and non-denominational churches, among others.

Catholic (73): 46 + 27. Adds Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees, plus longer Esther and Daniel. Catholics call these deuterocanonical.

Eastern Orthodox (~76–77): Greek commonly 76, Russian commonly 77, plus 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, Prayer of Manasseh, and Psalm 151 beyond the Catholic canon.

Ethiopian Orthodox (81, commonly cited): Unique books include 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and Meqabyan I–III. Scripture preserved in Ge'ez.

Translations are not canons: NASB, ESV, and KJV lean word-for-word; NIV and NLT lean thought-for-thought. KJV's 1611 edition printed disputed books separately; most modern KJV printings are 66 books. For full verse text here, use public-domain KJV / World English Bible by reference; other modern translations are referenced, not copied in full.

2. All religions, side by side — search and learn

Search a tradition, a scripture, or a practice. Every card shows scripture, teacher, core, practice, and afterlife — in that tradition's own terms.

Indigenous traditions are many and distinct — no single book, no single founder, and some ceremonies are closed to outsiders. That boundary is respected here, not filled in.

3. Cross-reference by topic

Pick a topic and see how different traditions approach it, row by row. Same topic does not mean same teaching — read each row on its own.

4. Compare two traditions

Beginner path, if you want to learn more about the Bible

  1. Genesis 1–3, Exodus 3 and 14 — creation, calling, and deliverance
  2. Psalm 23, Psalm 121, Proverbs 3 — songs and wisdom
  3. Mark 1–4, then John 1 and John 3 — the Gospels
  4. Acts 1–2 — the early church
  5. Romans 8, 1 Corinthians 13, Philippians 4 — the letters
  6. Compare canons last, not first — once the core story is familiar, the extra books make sense instead of confusing the start.

How to read a reference: John 3:16 = John, chapter 3, verse 16. Psalm 23:1–6 = Psalm 23, verses 1 through 6.

How every answer is shaped

  1. What you are actually facing, in one or two plain sentences.
  2. Best move first, with why it has the best odds.
  3. Other moves, ranked, each with most likely outcome, cost, and risk.
  4. What to avoid, and why it usually backfires.
  5. One next step you can take right now.

How probability is labelled

Probabilities here are reasoned estimates, labelled as estimates, using bands only — Most likely / Likely / Less likely / Unlikely — with the reasoning shown. They are never presented as measured statistics, and no fake percentages or uncited studies are used. If a fact is missing, the mode says what is missing instead of guessing it. If a situation is genuinely uncertain, it says so and gives the move that keeps the most options open.

1. Money — quick cash job

Situation: Someone offers same-day cash for a job with no written agreement, no set hours, and pay “when it is done.” A regular shift job is also available, paying less per hour but with a written schedule and a set payday.

Best move first: Written terms first, even for a small job. If they will not write it down, that refusal is itself information.

  • Most likely — Take the regular shift, and only take the cash job if it is put in writing first: job, hours, pay, and payday. Most likely outcome: steady pay on a known day, and a record if there is a dispute. Cost and risk: less money per hour on paper, but far less risk of working for free. Reasoning: a written agreement is the difference between a promise and proof. Cash jobs without writing are the ones most often paid late, short, or not at all.
  • Less likely — Take the cash job as offered, no writing. Most likely outcome: fast cash if the person is honest, but no way to prove the deal if they are not. Cost and risk: time lost, no record, hard to dispute, and no protection if the job changes mid-way. Reasoning: speed is the only advantage. Everything else about this move depends on trust with no backup.

What to avoid, and why: Working first and sorting out pay later. That order is how most of these disputes start.

One next step now: Ask for the job, hours, pay, and payday in writing before starting.

Likelihood bands are reasoned estimates, not measured statistics.

2. Street — late walk home

Situation: Walking home late at night. A dark shortcut saves ten minutes. A longer route stays on lit streets with other people around. A ride would cost money the person would rather save.

Best move first: Lit route, someone expecting you. Ride if anything feels off. Ten minutes is never worth the worst case.

  • Most likely — Take the longer lit route, phone charged, and tell someone you are on your way. Most likely outcome: arrive a little later, with far fewer chances of trouble and with someone expecting you. Cost and risk: ten extra minutes. Low risk. Reasoning: light, other people, and someone expecting you are the three things that most often keep a walk uneventful.
  • Likely — Spend the money on a ride if the walk feels unsafe at all. Most likely outcome: safest arrival, at a money cost. Cost and risk: money spent, but the safety gain is real, especially if tired, carrying valuables, or unsure of the area. Reasoning: when doubt is already present, paying for the ride is usually cheaper than the cost of a bad night.
  • Less likely — Take the dark shortcut, alone, to save ten minutes. Most likely outcome: usually nothing happens, but when something does happen on a dark, empty route, help is farther away. Cost and risk: ten minutes saved against a much worse worst case. Reasoning: the shortcut only wins on time. On safety, it loses on every factor that matters.

What to avoid, and why: Cutting through dark, empty ground alone to save a few minutes, especially with a dead phone and no one expecting you.

One next step now: Charge the phone, tell someone you are on your way, and take the lit route.

Likelihood bands are reasoned estimates, not measured statistics.

3. Work — sign or skip

Situation: At work, a supervisor asks the worker to skip a safety step to finish faster, or to sign a paper they have not had time to read.

Best move first: Do not sign unread, do not skip a safety step on a verbal order. Read it, or get the change in writing.

  • Most likely — Pause, ask for the paper to read, and ask for the safety step in writing if it is being changed. Most likely outcome: the job takes a little longer, but the worker is not on record agreeing to something unsafe or unread. Cost and risk: a short awkward moment. Far less risk than an injury or a signature that cannot be undone. Reasoning: a signature and a skipped safety step are both hard to undo after the fact. Slowing down before either one is the only move that keeps options open.
  • Less likely — Sign it or skip the step to stay on good terms and finish fast. Most likely outcome: fast finish today, but the worker now owns the risk if anything goes wrong. Cost and risk: injury risk, blame risk, and a signed paper that can be used later. Reasoning: being agreeable in the moment is the fastest way to lose leverage later.

What to avoid, and why: Signing to be polite, or skipping safety to be fast. Both trade a small moment of pressure for a large later cost.

One next step now: Ask, plainly, for time to read the paper and for the safety change in writing.

General information only. For a real workplace dispute, check a professional or a workers' rights resource before acting.

4. School — copy homework

Situation: A friend asks to copy homework, or to let the friend put their name on work they did not do, the night before it is due.

Best move first: Teach it, do not give it. Offer twenty minutes of explanation instead of the answers.

  • Most likely — Help explain the work, but do not hand over answers or a name on work not done. Most likely outcome: friend may be annoyed tonight, but both students keep their own record clean and actually learn the material. Cost and risk: a short awkward conversation. No academic penalty. Reasoning: helping someone understand is help. Handing over work is a shared penalty if it is caught, and the friend still does not know the material for the test.
  • Less likely — Hand over the work to keep the peace. Most likely outcome: peace tonight, but a real chance of both students being penalised, and the friend unprepared for the next step. Cost and risk: grade, record, and trust cost, for a problem that was only about one night. Reasoning: a one-night favour that can cost a grade or a record is a bad trade, and it does not fix the underlying gap.

What to avoid, and why: Copying, sharing answers, or putting a name on work not done. Short-term peace, long-term cost.

One next step now: Offer twenty minutes to explain the work, not the answers.
